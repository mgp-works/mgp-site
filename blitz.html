Blitz

Project overview

Blitz is a hackathon discovery platform that helps participants find relevant hackathons, evaluate past events through reviews, manage application deadlines, and find compatible teammates — bringing four previously disconnected tasks into one continuous flow. This was a solo project, covering desk research, user interviews, hypothesis validation, and usability testing.

Context and problem

The idea came from firsthand frustration as a repeat hackathon participant. Based on experience across four hackathons, I formed early hypotheses that participants struggled with three things: discovering relevant hackathons, finding trustworthy reviews, and tracking application deadlines.

My role and responsibilities

I independently owned the full project lifecycle: desk research, hypothesis formation, participant interviews, insight synthesis, information architecture, UI design, and usability testing. I also experimented with a GUI style inspired by developer IDEs for the visual direction.

Research and key insights

I started with desk research and prior platform analysis to define three hypotheses, then validated them through in-depth interviews with 4 participants (2 with 2 or fewer hackathons attended, 2 with 5 or more), conducted 1:1 in person or over video call. The research question was: how do hackathon participants find and prepare for hackathons, and what difficulties, motivations, and needs do they encounter along the way?

The interview findings both refined and redirected the initial hypotheses:

  • Discovery: The expected pain point was fatigue from visiting multiple sites. The actual root cause was cognitive load from repeated exposure to expired or duplicate listings, and a lack of hackathon-specific filters (e.g., participants wanted to filter by theme, such as AI-focused events).
  • Reviews: I expected users to want only the most recent reviews. Instead, participants deliberately cross-referenced reviews across multiple years because operating formats change annually, and they wanted to understand how an event's structure had evolved over time.
  • Schedule management: The assumed issue was poor personal schedule management. The real issue was structural — participants simply weren't seeing relevant recruitment posts in time (one participant described missing a deadline because they only saw an Instagram ad on the last day of applications).
  • Team building (new insight): This need wasn't part of my original hypotheses — it surfaced organically during interviews. 75% of interviewees were open to teaming up with new people, but lacked the information or platform support to judge compatibility, and organizers rarely provided structured support for team formation.

These findings translated directly into four product opportunities: a curated, deduplicated listing feed with theme-based filters; a review archive organized by year/cohort; proactive push notifications for hackathons of interest; and a team-matching feature based on tech stack and collaboration style.

Design process and key decisions

Team building as a scope decision

Since the team-building need emerged from interview data rather than my own prior experience, I treated it as a distinct opportunity and added a team-building community feature to the product scope — including in-app chat so users could start a conversation immediately after finding a compatible teammate, rather than switching to an external channel.

Iterating on the main discovery flow based on usability testing

I tested a prototype with 3 users and measured System Usability Scale (SUS) scores. The first round revealed that requiring users to register their topics of interest before seeing any recommendations created an onboarding barrier — users dropped off before reaching the feed, and separating the "all listings" view from the "recommended for you" view fragmented the browsing experience and added cognitive load.

In response, I redesigned the main screen so users land directly on a combined view — the full listing feed and theme-based curation are both visible and scrollable from entry, with no separate interest-registration step required. This let users browse casually even without pre-registered interests, while still surfacing curation naturally.

Visual direction

I experimented with a GUI style inspired by developer IDEs for the interface's visual language, developed independently alongside the interaction and information architecture work.

Final solution

Blitz brings together four connected flows:

Discovery

A themed browsing feed (e.g., medical, gaming, security, student-focused, environment, fintech, generative AI) plus filters by status, prize amount, region, and online/offline format.

Screens showing themed browsing (swipeable theme categories) and the filter panel (status, prize, region, format) — source PDF

Detail view

A consolidated screen showing everything needed before applying — eligible participants, application window, event dates (with calendar-add), venue, format, prize/winning benefits, and how to apply.

The hackathon detail screen showing eligibility, schedule, venue, and application info — source PDF

Reviews

A single place to compare participant reviews across years and cohorts, including short participant comments for a quick read on event atmosphere, and longer blog reviews organized by year.

The review archive screen with year/cohort tabs and participant comment snippets — source PDF

Team building

A collaboration-style quiz that surfaces a compatibility "type" (e.g., a diligent-supporter archetype), recruitment post recommendations based on saved hackathons, tech stack, and preferred hackathon type, and in-app chat to start conversations directly.

Results

  • Interviews clarified the original three hypotheses (discovery, reviews, scheduling) and surfaced team building as a fourth core need.
  • Redesigning the main discovery flow raised the SUS score by 13 points.
  • The number of steps required to reach hackathons matching a user's interests was reduced from 3 steps to 1.
